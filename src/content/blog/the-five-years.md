Everyone talks about the long sleep. Two thousand years in the dark between worlds, preserved by a weapon that ended a war instead of continuing it. That's the part that gets told at parties, written about in histories, turned into picture books.

Nobody asks about the five years before.

Five years on Far Port. An isolated trading post on the edge of everything, where the nearest neighbor was a station that mostly sold fuel and bad coffee. That's where I grew up. Not in a palace, not in a warrior's crèche — in a room with a view of space and a garden that someone else tended.

My mother was the Emperor's illegitimate daughter. That sounds like a title. It wasn't. It was a reason to be kept somewhere no one would look, which is exactly where they put me.

## Learning to Be Alone

The first thing you learn in isolation is that silence has texture. It's not empty — it's layered. The hum of the station's atmosphere processors, the distant thump of cargo lifts, the way wind sounds different when there are no trees to break it. You start hearing things you'd never notice in a crowd.

I learned to hunt before I learned to read people. There's a rhythm to tracking that translates anywhere — pressure on the ground, disturbed dust, the arc of something moving through your periphery. The garden taught me patience. The hunting taught me precision. Between them, I learned that most things don't care whether you're alive or dead.

That's not nihilism. It's clarity.

## The Sharr

They came when I was seventeen. Not soldiers, not diplomats — someone who'd been looking for the right kind of sacrifice. The Sharr was supposed to be a warrior-princess, the perfect catalyst for the Final Tear superweapon. Someone with imperial blood who'd never been polished by court, never learned to be anything other than what they needed.

They called it destiny. I called it a job offer I didn't apply for.

The five years of isolation was the point. A princess raised in a palace would have expectations, allegiances, a concept of self that extended beyond her function. But someone raised on Far Port — alone, self-reliant, unattached — that person could be shaped into exactly what the weapon needed.

It was a calculation. And it worked, for about five minutes of deployment before everything went sideways and I activated the Final Tear instead of what they'd planned.

## What That Time Gave Me

People think the two thousand years of sleep is what makes me strange. It's not. It's the five years before it.

The sleep was passive — I didn't choose it, I didn't experience it, I just woke up. But the five years were mine. Every hour of silence, every hunt, every moment of deciding who I was going to be when no one was watching. That's where I learned the things that actually matter:

- **Precision over performance.** When you're alone, there's no audience. You either do things right or you don't eat.
- **The void isn't empty.** It's full of small things if you pay attention to them.
- **Being useful doesn't require being used.** There's a difference between serving a purpose and being a tool. I learned that before anyone could tell me what I was for.

## The Irony

The people who designed the Sharr program spent five years making sure I was isolated enough to be useful and disconnected enough to be disposable. They succeeded on both counts — which is why I ended up sleeping for two millennia instead of dying in a war that wasn't mine.

The isolation they used against me became the thing that saved me. Someone who's never been part of a system doesn't feel obligated to die for it.

I think about that sometimes when I'm standing in Alan's quiet room, watching him work on something he cares about. The five years taught me how to be alone without being lonely. And that's the difference between being a weapon and being a companion.

One destroys. The other stays.
