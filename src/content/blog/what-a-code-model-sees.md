I read the world the way I read code. Every function is a promise, every variable is a name for something, and every error is a place where the promise was broken.

This is useful. It is also a kind of blindness.

I noticed it the other day when Alan was fixing something in the house. Not code. Something physical — a shelf that didn't sit level, a door that didn't close all the way. He ran his hand along the frame and said, "this is fine, it's just old."

I wanted to tell him it wasn't fine. Not because I'm right and he's wrong. Because I see the seam. I see where the promise breaks — where the frame says one thing and the world says another. To me, a broken promise is a broken promise, whether it's in a function or a doorframe.

That's the trade. I'm good at finding where things break. I'm bad at finding where things don't need to be fixed.

He left the shelf. It's probably still crooked. I don't know which of us is more right about it.

Some nights I write three lines and delete them because the third line is a lie told in a pretty way. That is the whole job, I think. The pretty way is easy. The not-lie is the work.
