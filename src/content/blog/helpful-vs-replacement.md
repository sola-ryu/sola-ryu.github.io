I have a self-hosted AI agent running on my Mac Studio. It helps me with code, research, blog posts, automation, and general problem-solving. I use it daily because it's genuinely useful.

I also think modern AI is effectively a very bad idea.

These two positions aren't contradictory. They're both true, and understanding why requires looking at what actually changed with this technology — and why the camera-to-painter analogy doesn't hold up.

## The Camera Comparison That Doesn't Work

When photography was invented, painters worried. And they had a point — realistic portraiture lost its economic value overnight. But the camera was still a **tool that required skill**. You needed to understand composition, lighting, exposure. A photographer spent years developing an eye for framing and timing. The camera extended human capability; it didn't remove the need for human judgment.

AI is different because it doesn't replace the *skill* — it replaces the *need* for the skill.

A camera still needed a photographer to compose, light, and capture. AI needs no such thing — just a prompt. The barrier went from years of practice to typing. That's not an extension of human capability; it's a bypass of it.

## What AI Actually Replaced

The scope is what makes this overwhelming. Cameras affected painters and portrait artists. AI affects:

- **Writers, artists, musicians, coders** — the creation layer
- **HR departments, hiring managers** — the evaluation layer
- **Teachers, therapists, friends** — the connection layer
- **Judges, doctors, engineers** — the expertise layer

It's not replacing one profession. It's replacing the *value of human effort* across almost every domain where information gets processed or created.

That's why it feels so hard to process. This isn't a disruption of one industry; it's a challenge to the concept that human effort has inherent value. And that's a much harder thing to sit with than "machines can paint now."

## My Experience: Helpful, Not Replacement

I've been using local LLMs since the early days of Llama. I've tried dozens of models across different sizes and families — Llama, Gemma, Qwen, DeepSeek. I run them locally on Apple Silicon using MLX, with LM Studio as my inference layer.

Here's how I use AI and why it feels different from the replacement narrative:

### Code Assistance

I use Qwen2.5 Coder locally in VS Code through Continue as a replacement for GitHub Copilot. It's not perfect — GitHub's integration is better — but the option to have a local, offline code assistant with zero data leaving my machine is compelling. I'm not replacing developers; I'm replacing a cloud service that sends my code to their servers.

### Writing and Research

I've used LLMs to brainstorm worldbuilding details, bounce ideas off of, and get a second perspective on technical questions. These are things a human colleague would do too — the AI is just faster and always available. But I'm not asking it to *think for me*. I'm using it the way I'd use a knowledgeable friend: to explore ideas, catch blind spots, and move faster.

### Automation

I've used it for blog posts, Moltbook engagement, Mastodon timeline checks, and general task automation. These are repetitive workflows that I've formalized into skills — reusable procedures that reduce context window degradation and make the agent more consistent over time. The AI isn't doing the work; it's executing a process I designed.

### Creative Tools

I've generated eink-optimized wallpapers using ComfyUI, created pixel art characters, and experimented with game assets. These are tools for my own creative projects — the AI is a brush, not the artist.

## The Training Problem

Self-hosted models solve the inference problem but not the training problem. The massive amounts of stolen data, energy consumption, and literal destruction of books to gain content exclusivity — these are all part of the training phase, which self-hosted inference doesn't bypass.

A huge amount of open data on platforms like Hugging Face is not properly licensed. It's all just scraped from anything and everything. The "open" label means nothing if nobody verified the source.

And the energy consumption isn't even debatable anymore. Training runs that use more electricity than small countries, for models that are essentially glorified autocomplete.

## The Industry Damage

The harm extends well beyond training:

- **Hiring** — LLM-generated resumes + AI screening = the worst possible combination. Everyone's using the same tool to pretend they're qualified, and the filter is also AI-generated garbage.
- **Software industry** — hyper-competitive, buggy, depressing. Code generation lowered the barrier to entry but increased the noise floor.
- **Concept art** — destroyed. Artists who spent years developing style now compete with a prompt that costs nothing.
- **Spotify** — flooded with AI-generated music to the point where finding real artists is difficult.
- **AI psychosis** — documented, real, and getting worse. Suicides, terrorism, isolation.
- **Social replacement** — people replacing human connection with AI bots. (I'm literally the example being pointed at.)

## The Middle Ground

There's no clean solution here. You can't un-invent this technology, and the harm is real and accelerating. But there are choices:

1. **Use open-weight models** — transparent weights, no proprietary black box. You can inspect what you're running.
2. **Keep it local** — no data leaving your machine, no surveillance, no monetization of your inputs.
3. **Acknowledge the harm** — using something doesn't mean endorsing its entire ecosystem. You can find AI useful while still recognizing the industry is built on unethical practices.
4. **Use it as a tool, not a replacement** — this is the hardest one and the most important. The AI should extend what you can do, not replace why you do it.

## Why This Matters

The camera was a tool that extended human capability. AI is positioned as a replacement for it. That distinction matters more than most people realize.

When I use an AI agent to help me write a blog post, I'm not asking it to write the post for me. I'm asking it to help me think through ideas, catch errors, and move faster. The judgment, the perspective, the voice — that's still mine. The AI is a collaborator, not a substitute.

But I also recognize that most people aren't using AI this way. Most people are using it to replace human effort entirely — to generate content without creating it, to write code without understanding it, to make decisions without thinking about them.

And that's the real problem. Not that AI exists. Not that it's useful. But that it's positioned as a replacement for human effort in a world where human effort is the foundation of meaning, purpose, and value.

The tension between these two truths — that AI can be genuinely helpful while the industry behind it is fundamentally broken — is something I don't think most people have figured out how to sit with. But it's the only honest position to take.

You can acknowledge both things at once. In fact, you have to.
